A little about me

From life sciences to AI products. Grounded in real clinical workflows.

I started in life sciences, moved through healthcare management, and found my place building the products that connect people, data, and better decisions.

Today, my work sits at the intersection of product management, UX, and AI. I've built for clinicians, hospital staff, payers, providers, and pharma teams, learning that the best technology starts with a precise understanding of the human problem.

Outside the brief. I experiment with a new tool or stack most days, and I pick up languages the same way, out of plain curiosity. In the work it comes out as wanting to know what someone is really after, and where a product quietly hurts to use. What I enjoy most is untangling a messy requirement into something a team can build. I tend to do it quietly: take the whole thing end to end, fill the gaps, and not make much noise about it. I make things by hand too, which is probably why I care how a product feels, not only what it does.

The method travels. What I do isn’t specific to healthcare: set the quality bar before launch, fix problems at the source instead of absorbing them, and keep a person reviewing what AI produces. It’s how I’d approach a product in any domain. My Splitwise teardown and my AI content work are where I apply it outside it.

The story

ChallengeBuilding a 3-story-point ticket took about 20 days from ticket-ready to done, and a defect spike traced to weak acceptance criteria hit as build volume peaked.

What I didI redesigned requirements-to-delivery around an AI ticketing workflow: Claude drafts, I review every story. I rewrote the acceptance-criteria standard and added AI code reviews and separate test environments.

OutcomeBuild time per ticket dropped and the per-release bug rate roughly halved in one quarter (metrics above), with a person still reviewing every story.

The hardest call

Keeping a person reviewing every story, even though it caps the speed gain. After the Q2 bug spike, letting the automation publish on its own would have traded a quality problem I had just fixed for a faster headline number. I kept the human gate.

What I’d changeStand up the AI code reviews and the separate test and UAT environments before the spike, not after it. The causes were visible in refinement long before they showed up as bugs.

Where Claude got it wrong

In a typical batch of stories, two to three drafts needed a real rewrite. The usual cause was reading the Figma link: Claude misread user flows (how a user lands on a screen or leaves it), or misidentified a button or sidebar component. Those are the details a human review against the design catches.

Stakeholders and communication

Who I worked with

Designers on the rebuilt BioMedTracker front end, product leadership, and customer success teams.

What worked

Sorting each group’s asks into needs and wants before they reached the backlog. Features with usability evidence moved forward; those without it were parked until we had some. Design, leadership and customer success could see where their request stood and why.

What didn’t

Needs and wants arrived mixed together, so priorities were debated late and some asks had no evidence behind them. I now separate the two in the first discussion and ask what usability data would settle it.

My part and the team’s

What I decided

To add AI code reviews and split testing and UAT into their own environments. Before committing, I discussed with engineering leads whether it could be done and at what scope, so delivery stayed smooth.

What the team did

Engineering built and fixed the modules, and the team delivered 7 of 12 in 5 months. The fall in the bug rate was the team’s result of the changes above, not something I did alone.

What drove ~20 to ~0.8 days

The process around Claude, more than Claude itself: a stricter acceptance-criteria standard, a human review of every story, updates after refinement, and separate test environments. Claude drafts faster, but the process is what kept the drafts build-ready.

The story

ChallengeClinicians opened Jeeves in Epic but ignored what it showed. Discovery showed why: search returned the wrong subject, so a clinician looking for “injection” got content on something else. Relevance was the problem, not the content.

What I didI owned the AI cycle: led discovery, defined the requirements and evaluation metrics, gated launch on precision, and balanced model cost and speed.

OutcomeAI recommendations and an in-app EHR chatbot launched across 15 hospitals (metrics above). Enterprise feature adoption rose 30% after launch-readiness work, which was broader than the AI feature.

The hardest call

Holding the launch behind a 70% precision floor while the first model wanted to recommend training for almost every encounter. Shipping sooner was the easy call, but a wrong first suggestion loses a time-pressed clinician for good, so I ran a two-week shadow deployment and gated on precision before anything reached users.

What I’d changeSet the precision floor and the shadow-run protocol before building, not after the first version came back too eager. The gate should be a requirement, not a reaction.

Stakeholders and communication

Who I worked with

Internal: engineering, data science, sales and customer success. External: hospital customers and clinicians, whose feedback and adoption shaped which features we built.

What worked

Giving sales and customer success a clear view of when to lead with which feature, and using customer feedback and usage data to settle feature decisions.

What didn’t

Pre-set tip-sheet templates. Training coordinators found them too rigid, so we moved to a tip-sheet builder they could edit.

In depth

How I ran it

I led 0-to-1 discovery with 20+ clinical and operations stakeholders, then owned the AI cycle with a 13-person engineering team. I defined the signals that make a recommendation relevant, wrote fallback logic for weak matches, and set requirements and evaluation metrics for the GPT-4o, Whisper and Vespa pipeline. I also drove launch readiness with Sales, Customer Success and Marketing.

How it’s measured

Precision was scored in a 2-week shadow run against a 70% launch floor. Response time is median end-to-end latency, cut by routing lighter tasks to the faster GPT-4o mini and serving retrieval from Vespa approximate-nearest-neighbour search. Cost came from that same side-by-side model evaluation. Retention is a relative lift among users of the new features across 15 hospitals.

From what to what. Precision +11%: the relative gain from the first model to the version I launched, scored the same way. Retention +5%: relative change in returning use, before versus after launch. Absolute baseline counts were not recorded, so I show relative change. Next time I would log them before the first release.

The story

ChallengeClaim status checks relied on manual payer calls, and transactions ran on legacy X12 EDI while healthcare moved to FHIR.

What I didI prioritised the high-volume claims backlog, defined requirements for automated real-time and bulk status checks, defined the integration APIs, and wrote the requirements and data mappings for the FHIR migration.

OutcomeClaim resolution sped up and millions of monthly transactions migrated from EDI to FHIR with uptime intact (metrics above), across 10+ payer integrations.

The hardest call

Migrating 10M+ live monthly transactions to FHIR without an uptime dip. The fast route was a hard cut-over; I ran EDI and FHIR in parallel for three to six months instead and accepted the extra cost and complexity to protect 99.9% uptime on traffic providers get paid on.

What I’d changeRun the CARC denial analysis before the migration, not alongside it, so the new FHIR mappings were shaped by why claims actually failed.

How it worked
In depth

The challenge

Veritable is a real-time insurance eligibility and claim status platform. Checking a claim meant manual calls to payers, and the traffic ran on X12 EDI, an older message format, while healthcare was moving to FHIR, a modern API standard. A wrong or late answer had direct financial cost for providers.

How I ran it

I wrote requirements for automated real-time and bulk status checks and defined the APIs for payer and provider-vendor integrations. I managed delivery of the EDI-to-FHIR migration, with its requirements and data mappings. I also used SQL on CARC codes, the reason codes insurers return when they adjust or deny a claim, to see why claims failed. Denials traced mainly to two causes: missing or incorrect claim information (CARC 16) and missing prior authorisation (CARC 197), so the fix sat upstream, in what was captured before a claim was sent.

How it’s measured

End-to-end claim resolution time before vs after automated status checks replaced manual payer calls. Volume counts monthly transactions across 10+ integrations. Uptime is held across the migration period.

From what to what. The 30% is the relative cut in end-to-end claim resolution time, before versus after automated status checks replaced manual payer calls. The absolute times were not recorded. Next time I would log them before the first release.

The story

ChallengeOnboarding meant 10 days of manual provider-data review, and recurring API ingestion failures frustrated clients.

What I didI replaced manual review with per-client validation checks, defined data quality rules engineering built in, and led the fix for the root cause of the ingestion failures.

OutcomeOnboarding dropped sharply, so a client could start sending data about 8 days sooner, and manual QA fell 20% (metrics above). For the health plans and systems, cleaner records meant a more accurate provider directory with fewer ghost providers.

The hardest call

Pushing the fix upstream to the clients' own data instead of absorbing it with more internal QA. Telling enterprise clients their inputs were the problem was the uncomfortable call, but internal clean-up would never have scaled past a handful of accounts. Clients accepted it quickly, because the ticket burden fell on them too.

What I’d changeBuild the validation checks into onboarding from day one, rather than retrofitting them after the ingestion failures showed up.

Stakeholders and communication

Who I pitched to

Our internal leadership team, with engineering, customer success and our data partners affected by the change.

The case I made

I presented it as a deck. Cleaning data internally would never scale past a handful of accounts, so I compared two options: more manual review as clients grew, or validation frameworks designed once and reused for every client’s onboarding. I recommended the second.

What I asked for

Engineering time to build per-client validation checks into onboarding, and agreement that clients fix their own data upstream. It meant telling enterprise clients their inputs were part of the problem. Leadership’s main objection was the cost of engineering. The frameworks avoided extra hiring and were reusable across every client.

How it worked
In depth

The challenge

Candor is an AI-native provider data accuracy platform that health plans and health systems use to keep provider information current. Clients sent their own data as health plan rosters and EHR feeds. The validation checks I defined sit on top of Candor’s AI intelligence layer. Onboarding a client took 10 days because that provider data was reviewed by hand, and recurring API ingestion failures kept generating support tickets. I owned neither the clients' workflows nor the data partners.

How I ran it

I owned client onboarding and replaced manual review with per-client validation checks. I defined data quality rules, such as NPI format checks, that engineering built into validation. I then diagnosed the root cause of the recurring ingestion failures and led the fix across engineering, customer success and external data partners.

How it’s measured

CSAT is a relative rise from client surveys and support-ticket ratings combined. Completeness is the share of provider records with all required fields, and the 10% is a relative rise. QA effort is measured per release.

From what to what. CSAT +15%: the relative change from the ratings before the validation checks to the ratings after, from the same two sources both times. The absolute scores were not recorded. Next time I would capture them before launch.

Prototype / ClarifyBuilding

Clarify: meeting and cross-project intelligence.

Problem
A PM can sit in 15 to 25 meetings a week. Each tool summarises one meeting alone, so a delay raised in one sync never meets the launch date promised in another.
Goal
Let a PM answer in under a minute: what changed across my meetings, and what needs my attention today? With proof for every answer.
See it working (synthetic data, tap a screen to enlarge)
Shows human-in-the-loop AI design · prototype on synthetic dataOpen prototype ↗
What I did as the PM, beyond prompting
  • Scoped it like a ticket. The first prompt set three sections, frontend only, mock data, no APIs, no login, and “do not add features outside the scope”. Why: prove the workflow before paying for AI.
  • Checked each build. After every round I asked it to confirm the sections existed and that nothing unneeded was added. Why: catch scope creep the way I would in UAT.
  • Sent it back. I told it the homepage was too close to my inspiration, and listed dead buttons, a duplicate button, pages that looked the same and too much copy. Why: AI builds what you ask, so the PM has to say what's wrong.
  • Wrote the specs first. I had it draft architecture, roadmap and design docs, then a product strategy doc, before building the core features. Why: decisions should outlive any one rebuild.
Decisions I made, and why
  • Go cross-meeting. Meeting tools summarise each meeting alone, but the risk sits between meetings: a delay in one sync breaks a date promised in another.
  • Evidence on every link. Each connection cites who said it and when, so a PM can trust it or reject it.
  • The PM decides. The AI finds and connects; it never changes a ticket or a date without confirmation.
  • Ruled out on purpose. Chatbots with no source, and any scoring of people's productivity.
  • Renamed it Clarify once the product became about cross-meeting clarity, not a single summary.
What I'd do differently

I wrote the problem and goal only after the first build, and I started with a single-meeting summariser. I'd write both first, start from the cross-meeting question, and add the single-meeting view after.
